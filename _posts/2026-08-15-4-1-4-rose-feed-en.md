This is the **4:1:4** rose feed — named for the nitrogen to phosphorus to potassium ratio it delivers (160 : 39 : 176 ppm, or 4.1 : 1 : 4.5 as elements). It is built around ICL Pekacid 0-60-20, which is popular with rose growers because it does two jobs at once: it feeds phosphorus and potassium, and it acidifies the solution. That makes it tempting to simply add more of it.

But there is a hard ceiling, and pushing ammonium up has a cost that is easy to miss.

**Move the sliders below and the complete recipe calculates itself.** Set your batch size, read off the grams, and check the warnings. Everything after the tool is just the reasoning behind it — you do not have to read any of it to use the recipe.

Every combination the tool produces is ion balanced, and holds potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphorus exactly on target. The targets come from the published rose feed values for inert substrate: NH₄ 0.7, K 4.5, Ca 3.4, Mg 1.6, NO₃ 10.75, S 1.6, P 1.25 mmol/L, at EC 1.6.

If you only take one setting away: **Pekacid 0.148 g/L, ammonium 10%.**

## Why the Sliders Stop Where They Do

The rest of this article explains the two limits built into the tool, and why the recipe reacts the way it does.

## What Pekacid Actually Contains

Pekacid is not a simple salt. It is a co-crystal of mono potassium phosphate and phosphoric acid (KH₂PO₄·H₃PO₄). Per **gram** of product it gives you:

| Delivers | Per gram |
|---|---|
| Phosphorus (P) | 8.45 mmol (26.2%) |
| Potassium (K) | 4.25 mmol (16.6%) |
| Free acid (H⁺) | 4.21 meq |

That free acid is what lowers your pH. It is also the part most growers forget when they balance a recipe — in an ion balance sheet, H⁺ counts as a **cation**, so every gram of Pekacid needs a matching extra anion somewhere else.

## The Ceiling: Phosphorus, Not pH

Here is the limit, and it is not negotiable:

**Maximum Pekacid = 0.148 g/L = 1.48 g per 10 litres.**

The reason is phosphorus. The rose target is 1.25 mmol/L of P. Divide that by the 8.45 mmol of P in a gram of Pekacid and you get 0.148 g/L. Go past that and you overshoot phosphorus, and **there is nothing you can do about it** — every other nutrient in the recipe can be dialled down by using a different salt, but once phosphorus is in the tank there is no salt that removes it.

To see how fast this bites:

| Pekacid dose | P delivered | vs 39 ppm target |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 g/L | 26 ppm | −33% (top up with MKP) |
| 0.148 g/L | 39 ppm | on target |
| 0.20 g/L | 52 ppm | **+34%, unusable** |

So if you were planning to step up in 0.1 gram increments, there is only one usable step: 0.1 g/L. The table below therefore moves in 0.01 g steps.

Excess phosphorus is not harmless. It locks up iron and zinc, and in a concentrated tank at pH 6 it will throw calcium phosphate — which, unlike gypsum, does **not** redissolve when you dilute it. It converts permanently and blocks drippers.

| Target (mmol/L) | 0.70 | 4.50 | 3.40 | 1.60 | 10.75 | 1.25 | 1.60 |
| Delivered | 0.68 | 4.50 | 3.40 | 1.60 | 10.73 | 1.25 | 1.81–1.91 |

### Why only three ingredients move

- **Calcium sets the calcium nitrate.** 3.4 mmol of Ca fixes the dose, and that dose drags in 7.48 mmol of nitrate and 0.68 mmol of ammonium whether you want them or not. Notice that the ammonium target is met by the calcium nitrate alone — you do not need a separate ammonium source at baseline.
- **Magnesium sets the kieserite,** and the kieserite happens to deliver exactly the 1.6 mmol of sulphate the target asks for.
- **Pekacid's free acid needs a partner anion.** Each gram brings 4.21 meq of H⁺, which counts as a cation. Carrying that surplus as sulphate (potassium sulphate) is why the last column grows, and why sulphate runs a little above target at 58–61 ppm instead of 51 ppm. That surplus ends up at about 2.7 mmol/L in the root zone against a 2.6 mmol/L root-zone target — so it is right where it actually matters.

### Which row should you use?

**The last one.** At 0.148 g/L you need no MKP at all, which is one less bag to buy and one less thing to weigh. It also releases 0.62 meq/L of free acid, which is roughly the alkalinity of typical borewell water after basic treatment, landing the solution near pH 6.

Lower rows release less acid, so your pH will sit higher and you may need to acidify separately.

You cannot weigh 2.4 mg on a kitchen scale, and you should not try. Make a **100× stock**: put ten times the above quantities into 1 litre of water, and dose 100 mL of that per 10 litre batch. Or keep using a ready micronutrient mix.

One warning specific to this recipe: **use Fe-DTPA, not Fe-EDDHA.** EDDHA breaks down below about pH 3.5, and that is exactly the pH right next to a dissolving Pekacid particle before it disperses.

## Table 2: Raising Ammonium to 10, 20, 30 and 40%

Roses need more ammonium when the root zone pH is climbing, and more nitrate when it is falling. So the natural next question is: how do you push ammonium up while holding everything else on target?

The recipes below start from the maximum-Pekacid blend, hold total nitrogen at 160 ppm, hold K, Ca, Mg and P on target, and keep the ion balance closed exactly. Ammonium comes first from the calcium nitrate (free), then from ammonium nitrate 34-0-0 (17% NH₄-N + 17% NO₃-N), then from ammonium sulphate once nitrate runs out.

**Unchanged in every row:** calcium nitrate **7.35 g**, kieserite **2.21 g**, Pekacid **1.48 g**, plus micros.

| NH₄ % of total N | Potassium nitrate | Ammonium nitrate 34-0-0 | Ammonium sulphate 21-0-0 | Potassium sulphate |

| 6% (baseline) | 3.29 g | 0.02 g | — | 0.54 g |
| 10% | 2.39 g | 0.37 g | — | 1.32 g |
| 20% | 0.07 g | 1.29 g | — | 3.31 g |
| 30% | none | 0.43 g | 1.47 g | 3.37 g |
| 40% | none | none | 2.85 g | 3.37 g |

## Going the Other Way: Can You Reach 0% Ammonium?

You can, but not with the calcium nitrate in the table above.

Ordinary Calcinit-type calcium nitrate is a double salt — 5Ca(NO₃)₂·NH₄NO₃·10H₂O — so it carries **0.2 mmol of ammonium for every mmol of calcium**, whether you want it or not. With calcium fixed at 3.4 mmol/L, that is 0.68 mmol/L of ammonium you cannot avoid, which is 5.9% of total N. That is the floor, and it is a property of the fertiliser, not a choice.

To get below it, two things have to change:

1. **Calcium nitrate tetrahydrate** instead of Calcinit. Same calcium, no ammonium.
2. **Magnesium nitrate** taking over part of the magnesium. This one is less obvious: at 0% ammonium the charge balance only allows 1.21 mmol/L of sulphate, but kieserite alone already brings 1.60 because the magnesium target drags it in. Some magnesium has to arrive as nitrate instead so sulphate can fall far enough. This is the source book's own instruction — *"replace magnesium sulphate with magnesium nitrate if less sulphate is required."*

The 0% recipe, per 10 litres:

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Calcium nitrate tetrahydrate | 8.03 g |
| Potassium nitrate 13-0-46 | 3.91 g |
| Kieserite MgSO₄·H₂O | 1.68 g |
| Magnesium nitrate Mg(NO₃)₂·6H₂O | 1.00 g |
| ICL Pekacid 0-60-20 | 1.48 g |

No Calcinit, no MKP, no ammonium nitrate, no ammonium sulphate, and **no potassium sulphate at all** — potassium lands exactly on 4.5 mmol/L from the potassium nitrate and Pekacid alone. It is the tidiest recipe in the whole range.

What it costs you is sulphur: **39 ppm against the 51 ppm target**, about 24% short, with EC at 1.53. That is not a fixable error — at zero ammonium the charge balance simply leaves no room for more sulphate.

Sulphur actually lands exactly on target at around **3% ammonium**, if that matters more to you than the nitrogen form.

**When would you want 0%?** Deep winter and low light, when roses cannot metabolise ammonium fast enough, or any time the root-zone pH is already falling and you need to stop pushing it down. The source book explicitly allows the range 0–1.5 mmol/L. Just note that the two products involved are ones you may not have on the shelf.

## Ammonium Nitrate or Ammonium Sulphate? Your Choice

The table above reaches for ammonium nitrate first, but that is a preference, not a requirement. **Ammonium sulphate works just as well**, and the third slider in the calculator lets you slide between them.

Here is the part worth understanding: **the delivered solution does not change at all.** At 20% ammonium, either end of that slider gives you exactly 160 ppm N, 112 ppm S, 176 ppm K and EC 1.78. Same feed, different bags:

| At 20% ammonium | Potassium nitrate | Ammonium nitrate | Ammonium sulphate | Potassium sulphate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate end | 0.07 g | 1.29 g | — | 3.31 g |
| Mixed | 0.88 g | 0.64 g | 0.53 g | 2.61 g |
| Sulphate end | 1.70 g | — | 1.06 g | 1.91 g |

It is not a straight swap of one salt for the other. Ammonium nitrate carries nitrate in with the ammonium, so when you drop it you have to put that nitrate back as potassium nitrate — and the potassium that comes with it then has to come off the potassium sulphate. All four ingredients move together, which is exactly why it is easier to let the calculator do it.

**Which end should you pick?** In India, ammonium nitrate is a regulated substance and is often difficult to buy in small quantities. Ammonium sulphate is cheap and available everywhere. For most growers here the sulphate end is the practical one — and it costs you nothing in the feed.

Two things to keep in mind on the sulphate end: ammonium sulphate must never be dry-blended with calcium nitrate (add them to the tank separately), and ammonium nitrate is strongly hygroscopic, so any opened bag needs resealing straight away.

At 40% ammonium the slider locks. There is no nitrate budget left at that point, so all the ammonium is forced to come from ammonium sulphate whether you want it to or not.

## Both Dials at Once

The obvious next question is what happens when you change *both* — a different Pekacid dose **and** a different ammonium percentage. That sounds like it needs a separate ammonium table for every Pekacid level.

It doesn't, and the reason is worth understanding: **the two dials do not interact.**

- Moving the **Pekacid** dial only changes Pekacid, MKP and potassium sulphate.
- Moving the **ammonium** dial only changes potassium nitrate, ammonium nitrate, ammonium sulphate and potassium sulphate.
- Calcium nitrate, kieserite and the micronutrients never move at all.

Potassium sulphate is the only ingredient both dials touch, and even there the two effects simply **add up** — the amount the Pekacid dial adds is the same no matter where the ammonium dial is sitting. So instead of a stack of tables, the whole design space fits in one grid.

Open **"Show the full grid"** in the tool at the top to see this directly. The number in each cell is the estimated EC. Read the grid left to right and almost nothing changes — that is the Pekacid dial, which is nearly free. Read it top to bottom and the colours run out fast — that is the ammonium dial, which costs you sulphur and EC on every step.

That asymmetry is the real lesson of this article. **Pekacid is a cheap dial. Ammonium is an expensive one.**

## Read the Warnings Before You Use the Bottom Rows

**40% cannot be done properly at all.** Calcium nitrate alone forces 7.48 mmol/L of nitrate into the tank, because that nitrate is how the calcium gets there. A 40% ammonium split at 160 ppm total N only allows 6.87 mmol/L of nitrate. There is no room. The 40% row above is the least-bad version: no potassium nitrate, no ammonium nitrate, all ammonium from ammonium sulphate, and total nitrogen forced 9% over target. The only alternatives are cutting calcium below target or bringing calcium in as calcium chloride, and both are worse.

**The published guidance caps ammonium at 5–15% of total nitrogen, with a hard limit of 1.5 mmol/L.** Only the 10% row is inside that. The 20% row is 2.29 mmol/L, the 30% row is 3.43, and the 40% row is 4.99 — more than three times the stated ceiling. These are short-term pH corrections for a root zone that is drifting alkaline, not steady feeds. Roses will show tip burn and interveinal yellowing on them, especially in low light.

**Sulphur is the release valve, and it climbs fast.** Every ammonium ion you add beyond the nitrate budget has to be balanced by a sulphate ion. That is why sulphur runs from 61 up to 182 ppm against a 51 ppm target, and EC from 1.61 to 2.06 against a 1.6 target. This is not a mistake in the recipe — it is the only way the charge balance can close.

**Never dry-blend ammonium sulphate with calcium nitrate.** In the 30% and 40% rows, keep the ammonium sulphate on the phosphate/sulphate side and add the calcium nitrate to the tank separately. Mixed dry, they react and throw gypsum.

## Practical Summary

- **Pekacid maxes out at 1.48 g per 10 litres.** That is a phosphorus limit, not a pH limit, and there is no way around it.
- **Use the maximum dose.** It needs no MKP and gives the right amount of acid for typical water.
- **Sulphur always runs a bit high** in any Pekacid recipe. That is normal and correct.
- **The two dials are independent.** You never need a separate ammonium table per Pekacid level — the effects simply add.
- **For seasonal ammonium adjustment, 10% is the sensible ceiling.** Use 20% only briefly and only in bright weather with a rising root zone pH. Treat 30% and 40% as a demonstration of where the chemistry breaks, not as a feeding plan.
- **Always dissolve to the full final volume.** Do not make a concentrate of this recipe — calcium phosphate that forms in a concentrated tank never comes back.
